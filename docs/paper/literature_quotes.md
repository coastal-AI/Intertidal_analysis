# Literature quotes for the MAREA manuscript

Verbatim passages from the papers in the author's collection, with page
numbers, extracted from the PDFs (pdftotext) and machine-checked as exact
substrings of the source text. Each quote carries the theme it serves and
one line on why it matters. Themes:

- **A** the flooding-curve / waterline method and its one-level-per-scene assumption
- **B** tidal propagation inside estuaries; accuracy and limits of global tide models near the coast
- **C** reported validation numbers and stated limitations (sampling, clouds, wet mud, ponding)
- **D** global importance, extent and loss of tidal flats
- **E** water-detection indices, thresholds, cloud screening
- **F** statistical origins (probit / logistic), sub-pixel mixing, variable projection / profiling

BibTeX keys refer to `marea.bib`. Use these passages to support
claims in the Introduction and Discussion; quote sparingly, cite densely.

## Set 3 — tide models and tidal prediction

### 2021_Hart.Davispdf.txt — `hartdavis2021`

**Reference**: Hart-Davis, M.G., Piccioni, G., Dettmering, D., Schwatke, C., Passaro, M., Seitz, F. (2021). EOT20: a global ocean tide model from multi-mission satellite altimetry. Earth System Science Data, 13, 3869–3884. DOI: 10.5194/essd-13-3869-2021.

**What it is**: The paper describing EOT20, the semi-empirical DGFI-TUM tide model (17 constituents, 0.125° grid) built by adding altimetry-derived residuals to FES2014, with tide-gauge and variance-reduction validation against FES2014, GOT4.8, DTU16 and EOT11a. We cite it as the source of our default tide model and for its own statements about where the model is and is not constrained (coastal data cut-offs, no uncertainty product, 230 validation gauges also assimilated in FES2014).

- (p. 1) "The EOT20 model shows significant improvements compared to the previous iteration of the global model (EOT11a) throughout the ocean, particularly in the coastal and shelf regions" — theme B: abstract's headline claim; the model we adopt is marketed on coastal improvement.
- (p. 2) "In general, ocean tides are known in the open ocean region to an accuracy of approximately 2 cm (Savcenko and Bosch, 2012); however, models show large discrepancies between one another and compared to in situ observations in the coastal region (Ray et al., 2011)." — theme B: authoritative statement that global models diverge at the coast, our motivation.
- (p. 2) "Poorer results are seen in the coastal region due to poorly resolved bathymetry, the complexity of ocean tides and due to land contamination of satellite altimetry radar signals (Fok, 2012)." — theme B: physical reasons why an altimetry-based model cannot resolve estuarine propagation.
- (p. 3) "For missions using the REAPER and TOPEX retrackers, a coastal flag is implemented that restricts the use of SLA data up to 7 km from the coastline. For missions using the ALES retracker, however, this distance to the coast is decreased to 3 km (Passaro et al., 2021)." — theme B: EOT20 contains no altimetry observations within 3–7 km of the coast, i.e. none inside our estuaries; the in-estuary tide is extrapolated.
- (p. 4) "For the M2 and N2 tides (Fig. 1), for example, the residual amplitudes can exceed 2 cm with the largest residual tides being seen in the coastal region." — theme B: magnitude of the correction applied to FES2014 concentrates at the coast.
- (p. 7) "the coast being defined as any tide gauges found shallower than 10 m, the shelf defined as being from 10 to 100 m depth and open ocean being anything deeper than 100 m. This is done to assess how the model performs in the coastal region, a historically difficult region to model accurately." — theme C: their "coastal" validation class is <10 m depth gauges, not intertidal or estuarine sites.
- (p. 8) "It should also be noted that 230 of the tide gauges used in this study are assimilated into the FES2014 model." — theme C: validation not fully independent of the reference model; supports our held-out-gauge design.
- (p. 12) "Furthermore, when compared to other global ocean tide models, EOT20 had the lowest overall RSS for the major eight tidal constituents, In particular, improvements are seen in the coastal region, where EOT20 shows a reduced RSS of 0.2 cm compared to the closest model (FES2014)." — theme C: the concrete number (0.2 cm RSS coastal gain over FES2014) justifying EOT20 as default.
- (p. 12) "In the creation of EOT20, a first look into the uncertainties of residual tide estimations was done, but due to the unavailability of uncertainty estimations from the FES2014 model used as the reference model these uncertainties are incomplete and, therefore, are not presented." — theme B/C: EOT20 ships no uncertainty field, so any per-scene water-level error is unquantified unless measured as we do.

### 2021_Lyard.txt — `lyard2021`

**Reference**: Lyard, F.H., Allain, D.J., Cancet, M., Carrère, L., Picot, N. (2021). FES2014 global ocean tide atlas: design and performance. Ocean Science, 17, 615–649. DOI: 10.5194/os-17-615-2021.

**What it is**: The design-and-validation paper for FES2014, the finite-element hydrodynamic tide atlas with ensemble data assimilation (34 constituents, 1/16° grid) that is the reference model under EOT20 and the model used by CoastSat/CoastSeg and DEA Intertidal. We cite it for its candid statements about coarse coastal mesh, a 10 m minimum depth, deliberate down-weighting of coastal gauges, sun-synchronous aliasing, and the explicit exclusion of estuaries and non-stationary tides.

- (p. 1) "The FES2014 atlas shows extremely significant improvements compared to the standard FES2004 and (intermediary) FES2012 atlases, in all ocean compartments, especially in shelf and coastal seas, thanks to the unstructured grid flexible resolution, recent progress in the (prior to assimilation) hydrodynamic tidal solutions, and use of ensemble data assimilation technique." — theme B: abstract's key claim.
- (p. 1) "It also provides very accurate open-boundary tidal conditions for regional and coastal modelling." — theme B: the authors position the atlas as a boundary condition for estuary models, not as an in-estuary solution; exactly our "mouth boundary" framing.
- (p. 5) "The resolution has been augmented to about 1.5 km in some specific places where coastal geometry is more challenging (such as fjords, estuaries, straits, etc.)." — theme B: best-case mesh is 1.5 km; our estuaries are narrower than that.
- (p. 5) "Despite the large increase in resolution compared to FES2004, the FES2014 mesh resolution is still clearly not sufficient in some highly complex coastlines, with narrow channels of dynamical significance, or topographically trapped wave generation sites, and it could result in a loss of details/accuracy in such regions." — theme B: authors' own admission for narrow channels.
- (p. 5) "Despite those efforts, bathymetry still remains unfortunately the limiting error to our prior hydrodynamic solutions in most of the global ocean, and also impacts the data assimilation accuracy in shallow-water regions." — theme B.
- (p. 8) "Conversely, Sun-synchronous orbits (such as ERS/Envisat/AltiKa) are disadvantageous in that matter: not only are the S1 and S2 tides projected on an infinite period (mean state), but many other tidal constituents show a rather large aliased period (see Table 2)." — theme C: the same sun-synchronous aliasing (S2 unobservable) that limits Sentinel-2 sampling.
- (p. 15) "A few additional members have been added from the perturbations of the model minimal depth threshold. It is usually set to 10 m in the T-UGOm hydrodynamic global ocean model." — theme B: the hydrodynamic model never sees water shallower than 10 m, so intertidal wetting/drying is outside its physics.
- (p. 18) "to avoid drawing the solution to fit some very local tide features observed by the coastal stations that may be inconsistent with the larger-scale tidal patterns that can be accurately solved at the resolution and/or bathymetry of the model." — theme B: coastal gauges were given 1 cm error (vs 3 mm offshore) so the atlas deliberately does not fit local estuarine tide features; the phase lag we measure is by construction absent.
- (p. 23) "A few tidal gauge sites show an increased variance but these TGs are located in very complex or enclosed regions and are thus not representative of the coastal ocean variability observable with a global ocean tide model." — theme B: enclosed sites are excluded from what the atlas claims to represent.
- (p. 25) "The nearshore performance, both in FES2012 and FES2014a, is probably limited by local bathymetry accuracy and coastal detail discretization, and ensemble/representers being less able to properly describe local error statistics, so data assimilation improvements in FES2014a propagate only partially toward nearshore zones." — theme B/C: nearshore accuracy explicitly limited.
- (p. 31) "we still face challenging issues in shelf and coastal seas, as well as in high-latitude oceans, where the accuracy of tidal atlases remains too limited for precise altimetry data processing." — theme B: conclusion.
- (p. 32) "As is the case for existing global tidal atlases, the stationarity of barotropic tides is not questioned, neither in hydrodynamic modelling nor data processing. However, it is quite a challenge for the future atlases in the context of SWOT, which will provide data in estuaries and deltas, and in very-high-latitude regions." — theme B: estuaries and deltas named as an open problem (river discharge–tide interaction, non-stationarity).

### 2025_Sutterley.txt — `sutterley2025`

**Reference**: Sutterley, T.C., Howard, S.L., Padman, L., Siegfried, M.R. (2025). pyTMD: Python-based tidal prediction software. Journal of Open Source Software, 10(116), 8566. DOI: 10.21105/joss.08566.

**What it is**: A short JOSS software paper describing pyTMD, the open-source Python package that reads constituent files from 50+ tide models (OTIS, GOT, FES formats) and predicts heights/currents at arbitrary points and times by harmonic summation with nodal corrections and minor-constituent inference. We cite it as the software through which EOT20 is evaluated, and for its explicit statement that it interpolates model constituents rather than adding any coastal dynamics.

- (p. 1) "pyTMD is an open-source tidal prediction software that aims to simplify the calculation of ocean and Earth tides. It is not an ocean or load tide model, but a tool for using tidal constants provided by tide models to predict the height deflections or currents at particular locations and times." — theme B: software citation; makes clear the accuracy is entirely the model's.
- (p. 1) "pyTMD is a generalized tide program that allows users to calculate both tide deflections and currents from a broad suite of models. Over 50 different models are presently supported, and additional model schemas can be defined with a JSON file." — theme B: why the same code can swap EOT20/FES2014/TPXO for our model-vs-gauge comparison.
- (p. 1) "It has also been leveraged within larger earth-observation software packages (Bishop-Taylor et al., 2025; Fitzpatrick et al., 2024)." — theme A: links pyTMD to eo-tides/DEA Intertidal and CoastSeg, the pipelines that assign one modelled level per scene.
- (p. 2) "For tidal predictions, pyTMD.io interpolates the tide model constituents to sets of spatial coordinates." — theme B: water level at any pixel is a spatial interpolation of the atlas grid; no in-estuary propagation is added.
- (p. 2) "The spatial and temporal components are then combined, and the output tidal time series is calculated through a summation over all constituents (Egbert & Erofeeva, 2002)." — theme B: the prediction is a pure harmonic sum.
- (p. 3) "It was designed for scientific and technical purposes, and not for coastal navigation or applications risking life or property." — theme C: disclaimer worth echoing for our product.

### 2025_Bishop-Taylor.txt — `bishoptaylor2026`

**Reference**: Bishop-Taylor, R., Sagar, S., Phillips, C., Newey, V. (2026). Optimising coastal tide predictions: an ensemble satellite altimetry and optical remote sensing approach. International Journal of Remote Sensing, 47(12), 5268–5287. DOI: 10.1080/01431161.2026.2666912. (File is named 2025 but the paper is dated 2026: received 5 Nov 2025, accepted 22 Apr 2026, published online 5 May 2026; cite as 2026. Do not confuse with Bishop-Taylor et al. 2025, the eo-tides JOSS paper 10.21105/joss.07786.)

**What it is**: Ranks 10 global tide models (FES2022/2014/2012, TPXO10/9/8, HAMTIDE11, EOT20, GOT5.6/4.10) around all of Australia by (i) Sentinel-3 altimetry RMS offshore and (ii) per-pixel NDWI–tide-height correlation from Sentinel-2/Landsat at the coast, then builds locally weighted ensembles validated on 95 GESLA gauges, and shows an FES2014-only intertidal DEM breaking down at Broad Sound. It is the closest precedent to our "pixel as tide gauge" idea, but it uses the imagery only to rank existing models on phase, never to estimate an in-estuary lag, and it names estuarine amplification/damping as an unsolved limitation.

- (p. 2) "However, existing tide models vary greatly in local performance, particularly in dynamic and complex coastal regions." — theme B: abstract.
- (p. 2) "However, tidal dynamics at the coastline can differ significantly from deeper waters, making it difficult to extrapolate model selection from offshore altimetry observations alone." — theme B: abstract; offshore skill does not transfer to the shore.
- (p. 3) "Previous coastal EO studies have almost exclusively relied on single tide models (e.g. Bishop-Taylor et al. 2021; Fitzpatrick et al. 2024; Vos et al. 2019), making the implicit assumption that a single model will perform consistently across their entire study region or even globally." — theme A: the one-model-per-scene assumption stated by the DEA group itself.
- (p. 7) "NDWI was selected as the optimal water index for the analysis due to its suitability for mapping exposed tidal flats, and its lower sensitivity to remnant water and wet substrate left in the landscape following ebb (receding) tides which can otherwise be misclassified as open water (Ryu, Won, and Min 2002)." — theme E: independent justification of NDWI over MNDWI for tidal flats and wet mud.
- (p. 7) "While this correlation calculation was inherently insensitive to absolute tidal amplitude due to an absence of directly measured water levels, it provided high-resolution insights into the accuracy of modelled tidal phase and the timing of tidal extremes predicted by each model relative to our satellite observations." — theme B/F: they extract only a phase ranking from the NDWI–tide relation; we go further and estimate the lag itself.
- (p. 11) "For example, while the EOT20 model outperformed all models across much of coastal southern Australia and offshore areas, it produced poor results in north-eastern Australia where other models like TPXO10 were more effective (Figure 3)." — theme B: EOT20 is locally best and locally poor; supports testing it per site.
- (p. 12) "The best performing ensemble models (ensemble-top and ensemble-mean-top3) achieved an RMS of ~0.166 m, a ~ 9% improvement over the best performing standard model (TPXO10 with an RMS of 0.184)." — theme C: residual RMS against gauges of the best single model is ~0.18 m even in Australia's well-modelled sites.
- (p. 13) "The best performing ensemble model (ensemble-top) produced an RMS of 0.287 m, representing a ~ 20% improvement over the best standard model (TPXO10; 0.360 m RMS)" — theme C: 0.29–0.36 m RMS at sheltered inlets/gulfs, the class our estuaries belong to.
- (p. 13) "At our Broad Sound, Queensland case study site, intertidal elevation modelled using a single commonly used tide model (FES2014) produced extremely low-quality outputs, with visible sharp elevation discontinuities and modelling artefacts visible across the intertidal profile (Figure 5(a))." — theme A/C: direct evidence that tide-model error propagates into flooding-curve DEMs.
- (p. 15) "the ensemble method resolves noisy intertidal elevation estimates and modelling artefacts (Figure 5(a)) caused by the single standard tide model (FES2014) being out-of-phase with real-world tides at this location" — theme B: the DEM artefacts are attributed to a phase error, which is what we estimate explicitly.
- (p. 15) "In contrast, our NDWI-tide correlation method is restricted to assessing how well a model predicted tidal phase (i.e. relative sequences of wetting and drying). As a result, a model could receive an erroneously high ranking so long as it correctly sorted satellite observations by tide height, even if it greatly misrepresented tidal amplitude." — theme B/F: limitation we overcome by fitting elevation and lag jointly.
- (p. 15) "This limitation is particularly relevant in embayed coastal environments like estuaries or inlets where processes of tidal amplification and dampening are common (Khojasteh et al. 2023)." — theme B: estuarine amplification/damping named as the open case.
- (p. 15) "A limitation of the remote sensing approach to ranking tide models is the reliance on sun-synchronous sensors. Satellites like Landsat, Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-3 overpass at the same local time each day, meaning they only ever sample a specific portion of the full tidal cycle for any harmonic tide constituents that repeat perfectly with solar days" — theme C: sun-synchronous sampling limitation shared with our archive.
- (p. 16) "In complex tidal regions where all models perform poorly, it will not be possible to obtain a useful ensemble output. In such locations, tidal prediction based on global tide models should be avoided in favour of observed local tide gauge data or more accurate intensive hydrodynamic modelling." — theme B: our imagery-derived lag is a third option they do not consider.
- (p. 14) "This high variability in model performance poses a particular challenge for coastal EO studies and operational workflows, many of which currently rely on a single tide model for image pre-processing and standardization" — theme A.

### 2024_Cui.txt — `cui2024`

**Reference**: Cui, H.-R., Lin, J., Su, J.-N. (2024). Multi-step-length gradient iterative method for separable nonlinear least squares problems. Kybernetika, 60(2), 197–209. DOI: 10.14736/kyb-2024-2-0197.

**What it is**: A numerical-optimisation paper (not remote sensing) proposing a multi-step-length gradient method combined with Golub–Pereyra variable projection for separable nonlinear least squares, tested on a complex-exponential fit and image deblurring. Only marginally relevant: citable for the variable-projection idea of eliminating linear/nuisance parameters analytically before optimising the nonlinear ones (the least-squares analogue of our profiled likelihood over per-band phase lag); if you cite the idea, the primary sources are Golub & Pereyra (1973, SIAM J. Numer. Anal. 10(2), 413–432) and Kaufman (1975, BIT 15(1), 49–57), both listed in its references.

- (p. 1) "Separable nonlinear least squares (SNLLS) problems are critical in various research and application fields, such as image restoration, machine learning, and system identification. Solving such problems presents a challenge due to their nonlinearity. The traditional gradient iterative algorithm often zigzags towards the optimal solution and is sensitive to the initial guesses of unknown parameters." — theme F: abstract; motivation for profiling out linear parameters.
- (p. 2) "The fundamental idea behind VP optimization strategy is to reduce the dimensionality of parameters by eliminating the linear parameters." — theme F: one-line definition of variable projection.
- (p. 4) "The separable structure presented in problem (3) allows for an efficient optimization strategy, the VP strategy, which was proposed by Golub Pereyra [18]. The underlying principle of the VP strategy is to reduce the dimensionality of the parameters by solving a linear least-squares subproblem of (3)" — theme F: attribution to Golub & Pereyra.
- (p. 5) "The VP strategy usually yields a lower dimension of estimated parameter space, a better-conditioned problem [30] and a faster convergence rate of the optimization algorithm [1, 4, 29]." — theme F: why profiling improves conditioning, the argument for our profiled Bernoulli likelihood per band.
- (p. 8) "The VP optimization strategy greatly accelerates the convergence speed of the algorithm and makes the parameter estimation more accurate." — theme F: empirical result.

### 2019_Bishop.txt — `bishoptaylor2019waterline`

**Reference**: Bishop-Taylor, R., Sagar, S., Lymburner, L., Alam, I., Sixsmith, J. (2019). Sub-Pixel Waterline Extraction: Characterising Accuracy and Sensitivity to Indices and Spectra. Remote Sensing, 11(24), 2984. DOI: 10.3390/rs11242984.

**What it is**: Evaluates a marching-squares sub-pixel waterline extraction method on synthetic landscapes (Landsat-8 spectra) and degraded WorldView-2 imagery, across five coastal environments, three water indices (NDWI, MNDWI, AWEIns) and three thresholding strategies (optimal, zero, Otsu). We cite it for the mixed-pixel argument (index value ≈ water fraction), for the non-linear response of NDWI to water fraction, and for the documented failure of SWIR-based indices and zero thresholds on wet tidal mud.

- (p. 1) "The sub-pixel extraction method shows a strong ability to reproduce both absolute waterline positions and relative shape at a resolution that far exceeds that of traditional whole-pixel methods, particularly in environments without extreme contrast between the water and land (e.g., accuracies of up to 1.50–3.28 m at 30 m Landsat resolution using optimal water index thresholds)." — theme C/F: headline result; sub-pixel information in medium-resolution pixels is real and exploitable, the premise of our per-pixel flooding curve.
- (p. 3) "However, applying these extraction methods to remote sensing water indices implicitly assumes that index values respond consistently and linearly to underlying proportions of land and water within a pixel, an assumption that has been poorly tested to date [40,41]." — theme F: names the linear-mixing assumption behind contour waterlines, which our sigmoid fit relaxes (only monotonicity is needed).
- (p. 5) "However, this index can produce poor results in intertidal environments where wet substrate remaining after high tide is often mapped as open water [19,20]." — theme E: why we use NDWI (NIR) rather than MNDWI (SWIR) on tidal flats.
- (p. 7) "When used to extract sub-pixel waterlines from a continuous water index surface, we make the assumption that low or medium resolution pixels along the land-water boundary represent mixed pixels, and that water index values (e.g., NDWI) for these pixels directly reflects the relative proportion of water and land within those pixels." — theme F: the mixed-pixel interpretation of NDWI that underlies treating each pixel as a graded (not binary) gauge.
- (p. 7) "As waterlines derived from remote sensing water indices can be highly sensitive to the threshold value selected to separate land from water, we extracted subpixel waterlines for three unique thresholds: an `optimal' threshold, a `zero' water index threshold, and an automatically derived threshold." — theme E: threshold sensitivity motivates fitting the whole NDWI-vs-level curve rather than thresholding each scene.
- (p. 13) "In a low contrast environment such as a tidal flat, NDWI values decrease approximately linearly with increasing land. However, in a high contrast sandy beach environment, even a small increase in land coverage (e.g., from 0% to 10%) can result in a rapid decrease in NDWI." — theme F: NDWI is a monotone but non-linear function of water fraction; supports a sigmoid/logistic response and warns of steepness differences by substrate.
- (p. 15) "In an extreme example, a `zero' threshold for tidal mudflat environments completely failed to differentiate between land and water for both the MNDWI and AWEIns indices due to the spectral similarity of wet substrate with turbid water in the short-wave infrared satellite bands" — theme E/C: wet-mud/turbid-water confusion in SWIR; justifies NDWI and a per-pixel adaptive midpoint instead of a global threshold.
- (p. 15) "Our results indicate that `Otsu' thresholding significantly improved the consistency of waterlines extracted using the sub-pixel approach, leading to error distributions which were generally narrower than corresponding zero threshold distributions, and more comparable across different environment types (Figure 4)." — theme E: reference point for Otsu if reviewers ask why we do not threshold per scene.

### 2019_Murray.txt — `murray2019`

**Reference**: Murray, N.J., Phinn, S.R., DeWitt, M., Ferrari, R., Johnston, R., Lyons, M.B., Clinton, N., Thau, D., Fuller, R.A. (2019). The global distribution and trajectory of tidal flats. Nature, 565, 222–225. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-018-0805-8.

- (p. 1) "However, owing to the difficulty of mapping intertidal areas globally, the distribution and status of tidal flats--one of the most extensive coastal ecosystems--remain unknown7." — theme D: tidal flats as one of the most extensive yet least mapped coastal ecosystems.
- (p. 1) "For regions with sufficient data to develop a consistent multi-decadal time series--which included East Asia, the Middle East and North America--we estimate that 16.02% (15.62–16.47%, 95% confidence interval) of tidal flats were lost between 1984 and 2016." — theme D: the key loss figure for the introduction.
- (p. 1) "This represents, to our knowledge, the first global estimate of the minimum total area of tidal flats. Our estimate indicates that, globally, tidal flats are similar in extent to mangrove ecosystems18." — theme D: scale of the ecosystem.
- (p. 1) "As a result, most remote-sensing studies of intertidal ecosystems have been conducted at local-to-regional scales, using satellite images acquired at known or modelled tidal elevations16." — theme A: the standard practice of tagging each image with one known/modelled tide level.
- (p. 4) "Our findings suggest that tidal flats are globally important coastal ecosystems that are declining in area, and which are highly threatened by human activities." — theme D: one-line justification for mapping intertidal topography.
- (p. 5) "Specifically, previous coastal remote-sensing methods relied on knowledge of tidal elevations at the time of image acquisition36–40, impeding the upscaling of analyses to the global domain." — theme A: identifies the dependence on a per-scene tide height as the bottleneck; our method estimates the in-estuary water level from the imagery instead.
- (p. 5) "Additionally, the sun-synchronous orbit of Landsat satellites and sparse acquisition schedule (every 16 days)42,43 can lead to under-sampling of the full extent of tidal flats in the Landsat archive and, therefore, the tidal flat map class should be considered as `observed tidal flat extent'35." — theme C: sun-synchronous sampling bias / missed tidal extremes.
- (p. 5) "Reference to imagery available in the online validation application indicated that pixels incorrectly classified as tidal flat typically occurred in areas of highly turbid water, polluted areas, aquaculture ponds and areas subject to strong wave action." — theme C/E: known confusion sources (turbid water, ponding) that also affect NDWI flooding curves.

### 2019_RobbiBissop.txt — `bishoptaylor2019ecss`

**Reference**: Bishop-Taylor, R., Sagar, S., Lymburner, L., Beaman, R.J. (2019). Between the tides: Modelling the elevation of Australia's exposed intertidal zone at continental scale. Estuarine, Coastal and Shelf Science, 223, 115–128. DOI: 10.1016/j.ecss.2019.03.006.

**What it is**: Builds NIDEM, a continental 25 m intertidal DEM of Australia from 30 years of Landsat NDWI composites binned by TPXO8-modelled tide height (ITEM v2.0 with a 306-polygon multi-resolution tidal mesh), validated against LiDAR, RTK GPS and multibeam (±0.41 m sandy beaches, ±0.39 m tidal flats, ±2.98 m rocky reefs).
